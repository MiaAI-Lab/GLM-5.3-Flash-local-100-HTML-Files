Vol. LXI .. No. 3,847 New York, Thursday, October 14, 1897 Morning Edition

The City's Crowning Work at Last Made Public Great Bridge Opened; Ten Thousand Cross on Foot

A structure of steel and conviction — the mayor cuts the ribbon at dawn, and the East Side finds its sky remade.

From Our Own Staff at the River · Illustrated

They began coming at four in the morning, in drizzle and gaslight — clerks with their hats set back, nurses with borrowed umbrellas, boys perched on the axles of ice wagons. By the time the sun found the towers, there were ten thousand of them standing shoulder to shoulder where for thirty years there had been only water and the argument about water.

The ribbon fell at 6:40. A carpenter's band played "The Wearing of the Green" badly, and nobody minded, because the bridge was walking away from them in both directions at once, a street turned perpendicular, planted in the river like an exclamation mark. The crowd made a sound that engineers do not have a word for.

A Tower of the Age

Each cable, the chief engineer said from the platform, could lift the whole of Broadway, "and would, if Broadway would consent to be lifted." Children counted the rivets aloud. A woman from Delancey Street touched the main cable with both hands and pronounced it warmer than expected, which the newspapers will print tomorrow, because it is true.

By nine o'clock the toll of footsteps had worn the plankway shiny. The toll collectors, expecting revenue, stood aside; the first day, at least, belonged to the public, and the public spent it generously, walking back and forth across the river for the sheer audacity of arriving.

What the Ferries Do Now

The ferry lines reported their smallest morning since the blizzard. One captain, moored and idle at the slip, was heard to remark that he had half a mind to row out and salute the thing himself, "on account of it being the finest orphan the city ever raised."

Late Bulletins From the River and Beyond Bridge Glows by Night; Ten Thousand More Expected

The electricians light the great cable at dusk — the city returns to see what it has built, and finds it hung with stars.

Evening Edition · Filed From the Waterfront, 9 O'Clock

At half past seven the electricians threw their switches, and the bridge answered. A hundred lamps bloomed along the main cable in a line so straight that men on the ferries stood up to see it, and a small boy on Pier 12 told his mother it looked "like the river had been sewn shut with light."

The evening crowds came differently — quieter, collar-up, in the mood of people arriving at a theatre. They found the towers washed in lamplight, the roadway shining, and the whole structure humming faintly under a wind out of the west, which the engineers insist is impossible and the crowds insist on hearing.

The Toll at Nightfall

Twenty-six thousand crossings were counted by nine. The coffee wagons did a brisk trade at both landings. A second ribbon — white this time — was cut at eight by the widow of the bridge's first chief engineer, who stood beneath the span her husband never saw finished and said only, "He always said evening was the honest hour for steel."

Police and the Prompt

The police, expecting revelry, found instead a city on its best behavior. Two pickpockets were taken near the stairway; both had crossed twice to study the crowd, which the sergeant called "the only honest ambition of the evening."

The lamps burn until midnight. Tomorrow, the newspapers expect, the bridge will simply be a road again — and no one who saw it lit will quite believe that.

The Wire of the World

PARIS. The Salon's autumn hanging drew queues around the corner; critics are calling it "a year of nervous light." — LONDON. Fog suspended all traffic on the Embankment until noon; the Aldermen blamed the river, the river declined comment. — VIENNA. A waltz of forty-one instruments was performed on a single floor above a coffeehouse, to the alarm of the coffeehouse.

City in Brief

The Elevated reports punctuality of ninety-one per cent, a figure the drivers celebrate by continuing exactly as before. A whale — "moderate in size, large in reputation" — was reported off Sandy Hook and confessed, on inquiry, to be a floating log. The Health Department advises cool evenings and warm suppers.
