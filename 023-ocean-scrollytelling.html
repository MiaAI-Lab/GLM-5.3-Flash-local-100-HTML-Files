Sunlight ZONE I

0 m — 200 m · the epipelagic

Where the ocean keeps its garden

Light still means something here. It pours through the upper water in shifting panes, and everything worth eating is green, quick, or both. Nine-tenths of all marine life never leaves this thin, bright lid — a layer thinner, proportionally, than the skin of an apple.

Color dies first with depth: red vanishes within ten meters, which is why blood underwater looks like ink, and why every fish worth fearing wears it without knowing.

Twilight ZONE II

200 m — 1,000 m · the mesopelagic

The hour of the vertical migrations

Twilight is not a place but a negotiation. Every night, the largest animal migration on Earth rises from here toward the surface — lanternfish, copepods, squid — billions of tons of biomass commuting toward food, then sinking again before dawn.

Here bodies learn transparency. What cannot hide behind distance must hide behind glass, and the water fills with creatures you can count organs through.

Midnight ZONE III

1,000 m — 4,000 m · the bathypelagic

No sun has ever reached here

Past one thousand meters, the idea of day is retired. The only light is manufactured: lures, alarm flares, blue sparks spat by comb jellies. Eyes grow enormous and telescopically tuned, or are abandoned entirely — in the midnight zone, both blindness and vision are strategies.

The water is near freezing and almost foodless; patience becomes a body plan. The anglerfish hangs motionless for days, spending nothing but intention.

Abyss ZONE IV

4,000 m — 6,000 m · the abyssopelagic

The largest habitat on Earth is empty

The abyssal plain covers more than half the planet's surface, and it is the closest thing the living world offers to another planet: cold, dark, crushed, and dusted with a slow snowfall of everything that ever sank. Life arrives in whispers — a sea cucumber every few meters, a dumbo octopus passing like a thought.

Snow falls here eternally: "marine snow," the memory of the sunlit surface, drifting down as flakes of the dead.

Trench ZONE V

6,000 m — 10,900 m · the hadal zone

The bottom of the world

The hadal zone exists only in trenches — scars where one tectonic plate slides beneath another. At the Challenger Deep, 10,900 meters down, the pressure is above a thousand atmospheres: the weight of a small car pressing on every square inch of you.

And yet: snailfish, pale and unbothered. Amphipods grazing on whatever arrives. Life drew no lower boundary here — it simply stopped needing one.

You have reached the bottom.

No diver ever has — not without a sphere of titanium and a great deal of courage. You did it by doing nothing at all. That is the ocean's oldest trick: it makes the impossible look like falling.
